What Is the Causal Effect of the Recentralization of Education Governance on Students’ Academic Achievement? Evidence from the Staggered Implementation of the New Public Education System in Chile

Fernando Rowland · University of Sussex

Session 2
Economics of education, inequality and well-being
Time
Wed 11 Nov, 14:45–15:15
Discussant
Professor Richard Dickens

Abstract

This paper examines whether the recentralization of public-school governance improves student academic achievement. While decentralization reforms have been widely implemented across countries to increase efficiency, accountability, and responsiveness in public service delivery, considerably less is known about the consequences of reversing these reforms through recentralization. Despite the growing policy relevance of recentralization, particularly in education systems characterized by uneven local administrative capacity, causal evidence on its effects remains limited.

I study the staggered implementation of Chile’s New Public Education System, which gradually transferred the administration of public schools from municipalities to centrally coordinated Local Public Education Services (SLEPs) between 2018 and 2029. Exploiting variation in treatment timing across municipalities, I estimate the effects of recentralization on student achievement using school-level SIMCE standardized test scores. The empirical strategy combines difference-in-differences methods, event-study analyses, and propensity score matching techniques to address concerns regarding staggered treatment adoption and non-random policy implementation.

The results suggest positive but statistically insignificant effects of recentralization on mathematics and reading achievement in the short run. These findings indicate that large-scale administrative reforms may require time before translating into measurable improvements in educational outcomes. The paper contributes to the international literature on decentralization, state capacity, and the economics of education by providing one of the first causal evaluations of educational recentralization. More broadly, it informs ongoing policy debates regarding the optimal institutional organization of public education systems in contexts characterized by heterogeneous local capacity and administrative performance.
